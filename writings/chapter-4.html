CHAPTER 4 · WORKING DRAFT

Chapter Four

Abraham would not live to meet all those whose lives would grow from his own.

There would be Isaac, and then Jacob. There would be children and grandchildren, marriages, departures, and households established beyond the sight of those who came before them. In time, the family would become a people.

But becoming a people did not make them any less a family.

Within that growing multitude, people still knew one another by name. They worked together, raised children, remembered those who had gone before them, and wondered what would become of those who followed. The promise given to Abraham reached into generations whose daily concerns he would have understood.

There would be years in Egypt, bondage, deliverance, and a long journey toward the land their ancestors had been promised. Children would hear those accounts from their parents and, in time, tell them to children of their own.

Through it all, the covenant remained part of their story.

Remember our scroll.

We have watched its human side grow crowded with generations. A household has become a multitude. Yet as we follow the writing, our attention begins to gather inward again. Among all those lives, one household comes into view.

Something in that movement reminds me of breathing—the living skin of our imagined ketubah widening and drawing close. We glimpse a people, then a family, then one person facing a choice whose significance reaches beyond anything she can see.

Let us come closer.

A Household in Bethlehem

The husband's name was Elimelech. His wife was Naomi. They had two sons, Mahlon and Chilion.

They belonged within the continuing story of Abraham's people, though they could not know what place their household would have in its unfolding. They had a home in Bethlehem, in the land toward which the earlier generations had traveled.

Then came a famine.

The larger promises remained, but the family faced an immediate need. There had to be food. Whatever discussions passed between husband and wife, whatever hopes or misgivings accompanied their decision, they eventually left Bethlehem and went to live in Moab.

We can imagine them expecting to make a life there, perhaps hoping to return when circumstances improved. The account does not tell us all they anticipated.

It tells us what happened.

Elimelech died.

Naomi remained with her two sons. In time, they married women of Moab, Orpah and Ruth. The household continued, carrying its loss into the ordinary work of living.

Then both sons died as well.

The woman who had left Bethlehem with her husband and sons was left without them.

What Could Be Restored?

Here the story asks us to slow down.

We have followed generations across centuries, but Naomi's losses cannot be understood simply as names disappearing from a family record. These were the people with whom she had shared her days. Her grief belonged to particular faces, familiar voices, and a future she had once expected to share with them.

What could she do now?

She could make decisions about the days remaining to her. She could consider where to live and how to find provision. But she could not call her husband or sons back into the household. Nor could she secure by determination alone the future that had become so uncertain.

Then she heard that there was food again in her homeland.

Naomi prepared to return.

The road would lead toward Bethlehem, but returning to a place would not make her life what it had been before. She knew that. When she spoke of coming home empty, she gave words to something no harvest could fully answer.

Yet someone walked beside her.

Ruth.

Within the grief of that household, another relationship had taken root. We will need to listen carefully to what this young woman says, and watch what she chooses to do.

For now, let us remain with them on the road: an older woman returning to the people she knew, and a younger woman approaching a country that was not her own.

Behind them lay graves.

Ahead lay Bethlehem.

And within the family to which Naomi was returning, there was a man whose place in their story neither woman yet understood.

His name was Boaz.
