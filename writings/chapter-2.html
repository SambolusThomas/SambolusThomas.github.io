Chapter Two

The First Home

Somewhere among the billions of stars in our galaxy, on a small planet circling an ordinary-looking sun, lies a world of extraordinary beauty. Oceans cover much of its surface. Rivers wind through valleys, forests rise from the soil, and living creatures inhabit nearly every corner of the planet. Water falls from the heavens, nourishes the land, and returns to the seas. Seeds become trees, trees bear fruit, and life continues in a cycle of astonishing complexity.

We call it Earth.

But consider it another way. What if this entire world is a garden, prepared within the vastness of God's creation? Not merely a little enclosure filled with plants, but a living world surrounded by an immense universe.

A home.

The Bible describes God preparing the Earth and filling it with life. Again and again, He looks upon what He has made and declares it good. Then, after creating the creatures of the land, sea, and sky, He announces something altogether different.

“Let us make man in our image, after our likeness." (Genesis 1:26)

Until this moment, the account has described the creation of a world. Now we discover that God intends to place within it someone who bears His image. Not another god, nor an equal to the Creator, but a being entrusted with a unique place in creation. Humanity is given responsibility for the Earth, instructed to multiply, fill it, and exercise dominion over the living creatures.

The scope of that responsibility is remarkable. The Earth itself is entrusted to humanity.

But the greatest gift is not the world. It is the relationship with the One who made it.

The Man Who Had Never Been Born

There was once a man who had no mother, no father, no childhood, and no ancestors. He had never been held as an infant, never learned to walk, and never sat at the feet of an older generation.

His name was Adam.

We have heard his name so often that we may have forgotten how extraordinary he was. Genesis tells us that God formed Adam from the dust of the ground and breathed into his nostrils the breath of life. He did not enter the world through the ordinary process of human birth. There was no pregnancy, no delivery, no umbilical cord connecting him to a mother.

Think of that familiar little mark on your abdomen. Your navel is evidence of a relationship that sustained you before you were born. Adam, having never been carried in a mother's womb, would have had no need for such a connection. It is a small observation, but it reminds us that the first man was unlike anyone who would follow him. What did Adam know? What could he perceive? How did he experience the presence of his Creator? Genesis gives us only glimpses of his extraordinary circumstances. God communicated with him, entrusted him with responsibility, and brought living creatures before him to be named.

We have no reason to imagine Adam as a helpless primitive struggling to understand his surroundings. I can imagine that if I were opposed to the whole plan I wouldn’t want you to know about Adam either. Anyway, we have enough information to describe the full measure of his abilities. He was the first man, formed directly by God and placed within a world prepared for him. No human being since has begun life as he did. And there is something else worth considering. Before Adam knew another human being, he knew his Creator.

That relationship was already established before the first human family existed.

The Gift of Another

Within the larger world God had created, Genesis describes a particular garden in Eden. It was watered by a river and filled with trees pleasant to the sight and good for food. Adam was placed there to tend and keep it. He had food, meaningful work, and a remarkable world to discover. Yet God observed something that He declared was not good.

"It is not good that the man should be alone." (Genesis 2:18)

The statement is startling. Throughout creation, God has repeatedly declared His work good. Now He identifies something missing from the man's experience. Adam was alone. The animals were brought before him, and he gave them names, but among them no companion corresponding to him was found. Then God caused a deep sleep to fall upon Adam and made the woman from his own body.

Imagine encountering another human being for the very first time. Not a stranger in a world already populated with people, but the only other person who has ever existed. We are not told what Adam thought when he first saw her, but Scripture preserves his words.

"This is now bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh." (Genesis 2:23)

There is recognition in that declaration. Here was someone who belonged with him, someone unlike any other creature he had encountered. She was distinct from him, yet they were made for a union so profound that Scripture describes the two becoming one flesh.

Consider what Eve meant to Adam.

She was not one woman among many. He had never known another. She was not someone he had selected from a world filled with possible companions. God had made her specifically as the companion corresponding to him. The Creator who had recognized that Adam was alone had supplied what was missing. And Genesis closes this remarkable account with an observation that seems almost too simple to deserve attention.

"And they were both naked, the man and his wife, and were not ashamed." (Genesis 2:25)

There was no shame, no fear of rejection, and no painful history standing between them. They had nothing to conceal from one another. They were different, yet united. Distinct, but not separated. They belonged together, and both belonged to the One who had made them.

A Boundary in Paradise

Among the trees of Eden, Genesis names two in particular: the tree of life and the tree of the knowledge of good and evil. God had given Adam permission to eat freely from the trees of the garden, with one exception. He was not to eat from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, and God warned that disobedience would bring death. One prohibition surrounded by abundance.

We might wonder why God placed such a tree there. The account does not explain every purpose behind it, but the boundary introduces something essential to the relationship between humanity and its Creator.

Trust.

Adam could accept the Word of the One who had given him everything, or he could disregard it. He did not need to understand every consequence in order to recognize the authority of the One who had spoken. The possibility of disobedience was real, and so were its consequences. For now, however, there was no shame between the man and woman, and no reason for them to hide from God.

Then another voice entered the garden.

The First Doubt

The serpent approached the woman with a question.

"Yea, hath God said, Ye shall not eat of every tree of the garden?" (Genesis 3:1)

Notice how the conversation begins. God had filled the garden with provision, but the serpent directed attention toward what had been withheld. Eve explained that they could eat from the trees, except for the forbidden one. Then the serpent directly contradicted God's warning.

"Ye shall not surely die."

Someone was misrepresenting reality. God had warned that death would follow disobedience. The serpent promised otherwise. But the temptation went further. The serpent suggested that God knew eating the fruit would open their eyes and make them like God, knowing good and evil.

Consider what was being offered.

The Creator had given humanity life, companionship, responsibility, and a world filled with good things. Yet the serpent invited Eve to believe that something better could be obtained by disregarding Him. The suggestion was that God was withholding an advantage, that His warning was not trustworthy, and that independence from His instruction might lead to something greater. The woman looked at the tree. It appeared good for food, pleasant to the eyes, and desirable for gaining wisdom. The promise was enticing, and she took the fruit.

She ate.

There is no description of mountains trembling or the heavens splitting apart. Genesis records the act with startling simplicity. But the woman had accepted another account of reality over the word of her Creator.

And something had changed.

The Man Who Was Not Deceived

Here we arrive at a moment that deserves more attention than it usually receives. Most people know the familiar account. Eve ate the forbidden fruit, gave some to Adam, and he ate as well. The entire event can be summarized in a sentence, and often is. But centuries later, the apostle Paul makes an extraordinary distinction.

"And Adam was not deceived, but the woman being deceived was in the transgression." (1 Timothy 2:14)

Eve was deceived. Adam was not. That raises a question we seldom consider. If Adam was not deceived as Eve had been, what was happening when he made his decision? Genesis does not tell us whether Adam witnessed the serpent's conversation. It does not record what passed between the man and woman before he ate. Nor does it describe what Adam may have perceived when he encountered Eve after her disobedience.

We must resist the temptation to invent answers.

But we should also resist the temptation to hurry past what we do know. Remember who Adam was. He was the first man, formed directly by God. He had no human parents, no childhood, and no generations of human experience to guide him. He had known his Creator before he had known another person.

Then God had given him Eve.

She was bone of his bones and flesh of his flesh, the only human companion he had ever known. God Himself had declared that it was not good for the man to be alone, and God had provided the woman. Now she had done the one thing God had forbidden. Try to imagine Adam's predicament without bringing thousands of years of human experience into it.

He had never experienced the death of another person. He had never watched someone he loved grow old and die. He had never stood beside a grave or endured the grief of losing a human companion. Yet the Creator had warned that disobedience would bring death, and Eve had disobeyed. What did Adam understand about what had happened to her?

Did he recognize a change that we cannot begin to describe? Had something within their extraordinary unity been disturbed? Could he perceive a separation that had never existed before?

Imagine two things once together beginning to draw apart, like oil separating from water. We cannot know whether Adam experienced anything resembling that image, but it offers a way to contemplate the possibility that he recognized something profoundly different in Eve. Whatever Adam perceived, the moment was unlike anything we have ever experienced. The woman who was his own flesh had crossed a boundary he knew God had established. She had believed the serpent. He had not.

What would become of her? What would become of their union? What did the warning of death mean for the only person with whom Adam had shared human companionship?

We stand on the other side of history, knowing the suffering that followed and the promises God would later reveal. Adam possessed none of that history. Before we confidently declare what we would have done differently, we ought to consider what it would mean to stand in his place. Yet one thing had not changed.

God was still God.

The Creator who had formed Adam and given him Eve had not ceased to be wise, powerful, or merciful because the woman had disobeyed. Whatever had happened, the One who had provided everything was still there. Could Adam have entrusted Eve's future to Him? Was there a way forward that Adam could not see?

Scripture does not tell us what God might have done had Adam refused the fruit. We cannot know the answer, but neither should we imagine that the Creator had suddenly become helpless.

Now return to Adam.

The woman stands before him. She has eaten, and he knows what God has said. Whatever thoughts and emotions filled that moment, he faced a decision whose consequences he could not fully comprehend. Perhaps we would have chosen differently. But would we? Adam made his choice.

He ate.

The consequences would reach every generation of humanity that followed.

The Terrible Discovery

The serpent had promised that their eyes would be opened, and Genesis tells us they were. But what did they discover?

They knew they were naked.

The man and woman who had been naked and unashamed now gathered fig leaves and made coverings for themselves. The knowledge they had sought did not bring the satisfaction they had imagined. It brought shame. When they heard the LORD God in the garden, they hid themselves among the trees. Try to consider that reversal.

The Creator had made the Earth their home, filled it with life, and prepared a place where humanity could know Him. Now the trees that once provided beauty and nourishment had become hiding places from the One who made them.

Something had changed in their relationship with God, and the change was terrible. Then God called to Adam.

"Where art thou?" (Genesis 3:9)

Surely the Creator of the heavens and the Earth did not need directions to find a man hiding among the trees. The question reaches beyond Adam's physical location. The man who had known fellowship with God was now afraid to encounter Him.

God came looking.

Adam explained that he had heard God's voice and was afraid because he was naked. When God asked whether he had eaten from the forbidden tree, Adam pointed to the woman, even reminding God that He had given her to him. The woman, in turn, pointed to the serpent. The change in their relationships is heartbreaking. The man who had once joyfully declared the woman to be bone of his bones and flesh of his flesh now named her in his explanation of disobedience. Shame had entered their experience, and blame had entered their conversation. Yet another detail deserves our attention.

God was still speaking to them.

He did not pretend their choices were inconsequential. He confronted what had happened and declared the consequences that would follow. But the relationship had not ceased to matter to Him. The man and woman had hidden themselves.

God had come looking for them.

The Cost of Separation

The consequences of their choices were terrible. Human life would now be marked by suffering, difficult labor, conflict, and death. The ground would bring forth thorns and thistles, and Adam would eat bread by the sweat of his face until he returned to the dust from which he had been formed.

"For dust thou art, and unto dust shalt thou return." (Genesis 3:19)

Adam had been made from the substance of the Earth, and now the Earth would receive him again. The world God had prepared remained, but humanity's experience of it had changed profoundly. The rivers would continue flowing, the plants growing, and the creatures multiplying. Yet human life within that world would no longer be what it had been. Centuries later, Paul would describe creation itself as groaning under corruption and awaiting deliverance (Romans 8:19–23). The consequences of humanity's fall reached beyond Eden, and the hope of redemption would eventually be described on that same vast scale. The Earth had been prepared as humanity's home. And the story of its restoration was far from finished.

The Covering

Before Adam and Eve left Eden, something remarkable occurred. They had attempted to cover themselves with fig leaves, but Genesis records another act.

"Unto Adam also and to his wife did the LORD God make coats of skins, and clothed them." (Genesis 3:21)

God clothed them. The One whose command they had disobeyed provided for them. Their choices had consequences that could not simply be dismissed, yet God continued to care for the creatures He had made. We are not told everything involved in the making of those garments. But we are told who provided them. The Creator did which is a detail worth remembering.

Humanity had attempted to conceal its condition. God provided a covering.

As the story unfolds, we will encounter that pattern again, in circumstances far removed from the first garden.

The Promise

Before the man and woman leave Eden, God speaks words to the serpent that introduce another mystery. There will be hostility between the serpent and the woman, and between their offspring. Then comes a declaration of conflict:

the woman's offspring will strike the serpent's head, while the serpent will strike his heel (Genesis 3:15).

Who is this offspring? How will the conflict unfold, and what will become of the serpent? Genesis does not immediately provide the answers. But the declaration introduces the prospect of a victory that lies beyond the events in Eden. Christians have long understood these words as the earliest anticipation of the Redeemer, though the fullness of that interpretation emerges only as the biblical story continues.

Remember what we encountered in the previous chapter. Peter and Paul described God's purpose in Christ as reaching back before the foundation of the world. Now, near the beginning of human history, we find a promise that will become increasingly significant as the generations unfold. The human story has barely begun, yet it is already moving toward something beyond itself.

The serpent will not have the final word.

Outside the First Home

Adam and Eve leave Eden. The way to the tree of life is guarded, and they cannot simply return to their former condition as though nothing has happened. But they remain upon the Earth, the extraordinary world God had prepared. They will have children, and their descendants will cultivate fields, develop skills, build cities, establish nations, and spread across the land. Human history will fill with remarkable accomplishments and terrible failures. There is no means by which we can measure or understand what was lost in the Garden. I am asking…

Some will seek God. Others will turn away.

Generation after generation, humanity will attempt to establish happiness, security, and peace within a world it no longer experiences as it once did. Yet no amount of human achievement can, by itself, restore what was lost in Eden. Building a more comfortable home is not the same as repairing a broken relationship with the One who made it.And here we should remember something important. The Creator had not ceased to love the creatures who turned away from Him. He had confronted their disobedience, provided a covering, and spoken a promise whose meaning would reach far beyond their lifetimes.

The first home, as humanity had known it, was lost. The consequences were real, and every generation that followed would encounter them. The story of God's love had not ended. Remember the two sides of our scroll. On one side is humanity's struggle, with its failures, suffering, and repeated attempts to find a way forward. On the other is God's purpose, already present before the foundation of the world. The two accounts are inseparable.

Yet somewhere within the unfolding generations, the promise spoken in Eden will begin to take shape. The Earth was made to be humanity's home, but humanity had lost something greater than a place to live. It had lost the fellowship for which it was created. The question now was not whether mankind could build another garden. It was whether the relationship broken in the first one could ever be restored which raises a far more extraordinary question.

What would God be willing to do to bring His children home?
